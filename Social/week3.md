### **Module 1: Social Stratification**

#### **Narrative Lecture:**

**Introduction to Social Stratification**

Welcome to Module 1 of our lecture on social problems. Today, we will delve into the concept of social stratification. **Social stratification** refers to the structured inequalities between different social groups within a society. It involves the hierarchical arrangement of individuals based on various attributes such as property, occupation, gender, age, and religious affiliation.

Social stratification can be visualized as layers or strata within a society, where individuals and groups have unequal access to resources, opportunities, and rewards based on their position within this hierarchy. Those at the top of the hierarchy typically enjoy greater privileges, power, and wealth, while those at the bottom face more disadvantages.

The concept of **social class**, also known as socio-economic class, is a key component of social stratification. Social class refers to large-scale groupings of people who share similar economic resources and social status, which strongly influence their lifestyles, life chances, and social mobility.

**Social mobility** is the ability of individuals or groups to move up or down the social hierarchy. It is a measure of the degree to which a society allows for changes in social status based on factors like education, occupation, and income. In societies with high social mobility, individuals have greater opportunities to improve their socio-economic position through their efforts and achievements.

**Operationalising Social Class**

To study social class, sociologists need to operationalize this abstract concept, transforming it into measurable variables. **Operationalising** involves defining social class clearly and concretely enough to be tested in empirical research. One common method is to use occupation as an indicator of social class.

Occupation serves as a practical and widely accepted measure because individuals in the same occupation often experience similar degrees of social advantage or disadvantage, share similar lifestyles, and have comparable life opportunities. For instance, a doctor and a lawyer might both be considered part of the middle class due to their professional status, income levels, and social influence.

Several schemes have been developed to map the class structure of society. These schemes provide theoretical frameworks that allocate individuals to social class categories based on their occupation and other relevant factors.

**Measuring Social Mobility**

One influential approach to measuring social mobility is the **EGP (Erikson-Goldthorpe-Portocarero) schema**, developed by British sociologist John Goldthorpe and his colleagues. The EGP schema, also known as the Goldthorpe Class Schema, is an example of an employment aggregate approach to class analysis. It identifies class location based on two main factors: market situation and work situation.

- **Market situation** refers to material rewards and life chances, including level of pay, job security, and prospects for advancement.
- **Work situation** involves control, power, authority, and status at work.

Goldthorpe's work has been highly influential, generating the most widely used occupation-based social classification in sociology and allied disciplines. It has been employed in various empirical research projects, including the CASMIN (Comparative Analysis of Social Mobility in Industrial Nations) project, which had significant practical results. The CASMIN classification of occupational class has been adopted by the UK Office of National Statistics and is widely used across Europe.

The Goldthorpe approach has its advantages, such as the availability of data from censuses and its usefulness in correlating certain inequalities, like health outcomes, to class. However, it also faces criticisms, including its exclusion of major capital holders and the long-term unemployed, as well as its lack of consideration for class consciousness and self-identity.

**Historical Context**

Understanding the historical context of social stratification is essential for comprehending its current manifestations. Post-1945, the creation of the welfare state and the growth of bureaucracies in Western societies led to significant changes in the class structure. There was a notable shift where professionals such as doctors and lawyers moved from self-employment to working within institutional environments.

Additionally, there was an enormous growth in welfare professions, including teaching, healthcare, and social work. Modern economic development created an ever-rising demand for experts in law, finance, accounting, technology, and information systems. As a result, the middle class expanded significantly, accounting for a majority of the population in Britain and other industrialized countries. The rise of the middle class was largely due to the increasing proportion of white-collar jobs relative to blue-collar jobs over the 20th century.

In contrast, social stratification in the USSR and post-Soviet societies followed a different trajectory. The USSR aimed to create a classless society by eliminating the private ownership of production means. However, a new form of stratification emerged based on political power and party affiliation. After the dissolution of the USSR, post-Soviet societies developed new systems of socio-economic class, influenced by market reforms and the reintroduction of private property.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, social stratification is a fundamental concept in sociology that helps us understand the structured inequalities within societies. By examining social class, social mobility, and the operationalisation of these concepts, we gain insights into how individuals and groups are positioned within social hierarchies. Understanding the historical context of social stratification further enriches our comprehension of its development and current state.

### **Module 2: Poverty**

#### **Narrative Lecture:**

**Introduction to Poverty**

Welcome to Module 2 of our lecture on social problems. Today, we will explore the complex and pressing issue of poverty. Poverty is a significant social problem that affects millions of people worldwide. It refers to a condition where individuals or groups lack the financial resources to meet basic living needs such as food, shelter, and healthcare.

Poverty is more than just an **economic condition**; it is a multidimensional issue that impacts various aspects of life, including **health**, **education**, and **social participation**. It is crucial to understand poverty not only as a lack of income but also as a deprivation of opportunities and capabilities. When discussing poverty, we often refer to both its objective conditions and its subjective experiences, emphasizing the need for comprehensive strategies to address it.

**Types of Poverty**

To gain a deeper understanding of poverty, it is essential to distinguish between **absolute** poverty and **relative** poverty. These two concepts provide different perspectives on the nature and measurement of poverty.

**Absolute Poverty** **is defined by the inability to meet basic physical needs required for survival, such as food, clean water, clothing, and shelter.** It is a condition characterized by severe deprivation, where individuals struggle to maintain life and health. **Absolute poverty is often measured by setting a fixed poverty line, such as the international poverty line set by the World Bank at \$1.90 per day**. Individuals living **below this line are considered to be in absolute poverty.**

**An example of absolute poverty can be seen in communities with limited access to clean drinking water or adequate nutrition, leading to high rates of malnutrition and preventable diseases.** Absolute poverty is often prevalent in less developed regions and is a critical focus for global humanitarian efforts.

**Relative Poverty**, on the other hand, **is defined in relation to the standards of living in a specific society at a particular time.** It takes into account social and cultural needs in addition to basic physical needs. **Relative poverty involves being deprived of the opportunities, comforts, and self-respect that the majority of people in the society enjoy.** This concept was extensively discussed by sociologist Peter Townsend in 1979, who argued that **poverty should be understood in terms of social exclusion and inability to participate fully in society.**

**For instance, in a wealthy country, a family may not be able to afford to buy a computer or internet access, which limits their children's ability to complete school assignments and access educational resources.** This deprivation can hinder social mobility and perpetuate cycles of poverty.

Both absolute and relative poverty have their advantages and criticisms. **Absolute poverty** **provides a clear and easily measurable standard, making it useful for international comparisons and policy interventions aimed at addressing basic survival needs.** However, it may overlook the broader social and cultural dimensions of poverty that affect people's well-being and quality of life.

**Relative poverty** **emphasizes the importance of social inclusion and the ability to participate in society, highlighting the dynamic and context-specific nature of poverty.** However, it can be more challenging to measure and may vary significantly across different societies and time periods.

**Social Exclusion and Poverty**

Understanding social exclusion is crucial when discussing relative poverty. **Social exclusion refers to the process by which individuals or groups are marginalized and denied full participation in the social, economic, and political life of their communities.** It is both a cause and a consequence of poverty, creating a vicious cycle that perpetuates disadvantage.

**People experiencing relative poverty often face social exclusion**, which manifests in various forms, such as lack of access to **education**, **employment**, **healthcare**, and **social networks**. **This exclusion can lead to feelings of isolation, low self-esteem, and a sense of powerlessness.**

Peter Townsend's concept of **relative poverty** is closely linked to **social exclusion**. He defined poverty as the lack of resources to obtain the types of **diets**, participate in the **activities**, and have the **living conditions** and **amenities** that are customary or widely accepted in the society to which one belongs. Townsend's research highlighted that individuals in relative poverty are effectively excluded from ordinary living patterns, customs, and activities, which are fundamental to social integration and participation.

The **consensual idea of poverty** involves asking the public what they regard as being necessary for a minimum standard of living. This approach aims to establish a widespread agreement on what constitutes poverty based on contemporary standards of living. **For example, a consensual survey might identify essential items and activities that people believe everyone should have access to, such as adequate housing, healthcare, education, and social participation.**

Using this consensual approach, researchers can develop a deprivation index to measure poverty in relation to the standards and expectations of the society. **This method considers public perceptions and helps to create a more comprehensive and culturally relevant understanding of poverty.**

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, poverty is a complex and multifaceted social problem that requires a nuanced understanding of its various dimensions. By distinguishing between absolute and relative poverty, we can appreciate the different ways in which poverty affects individuals and communities. Understanding social exclusion in the context of relative poverty and considering the consensual idea of poverty allows us to develop more effective and culturally sensitive strategies to address this pervasive issue.

### **Module 3: Inequality**

#### **Narrative Lecture:**

**Introduction to Inequality**

Welcome to Module 3 of our lecture on social problems. Today, we will explore the concept of inequality. Inequality refers to the unequal distribution of resources, opportunities, and privileges among individuals and groups within a society. It encompasses disparities in income, wealth, education, health, and access to services, among other factors.

Understanding inequality is crucial because it affects various aspects of life, influencing social cohesion, economic stability, and individual well-being. High levels of inequality can lead to social unrest, reduced economic growth, and increased health problems. It is essential to examine both the causes and consequences of inequality to develop strategies for creating a more equitable society.

**Measuring Inequality**

To measure inequality, sociologists and economists use various indicators. One of the most widely used measures is the **Gini coefficient**. The Gini coefficient is a numerical measure of income inequality within a population, ranging from 0 to 1. A Gini coefficient of 0 represents perfect equality, where everyone has the same income, while a Gini coefficient of 1 indicates perfect inequality, where one person has all the income, and everyone else has none.

The Gini coefficient is calculated based on the Lorenz curve, which plots the cumulative percentage of total income received by the cumulative percentage of the population. The further the Lorenz curve is from the line of perfect equality, the higher the Gini coefficient and the greater the inequality.

For example, a Gini coefficient of 0.3 indicates a relatively equitable distribution of income, while a Gini coefficient of 0.5 suggests a higher level of income inequality. The Gini coefficient allows for comparisons of inequality across different countries and time periods, providing valuable insights into trends and patterns of income distribution.

Another measure of inequality is the **income quintile ratio**, which compares the income share of the richest 20% of the population to that of the poorest 20%. This ratio provides a straightforward indication of the income gap between the wealthiest and the poorest segments of society. A higher ratio indicates greater inequality.

**Poverty rates** and **median income** are additional indicators used to assess inequality. Poverty rates measure the percentage of the population living below a defined poverty line, while median income represents the middle point of income distribution, highlighting the standard of living of the typical household.

**Inequality as a Social Problem**

There is ongoing debate about whether inequality should be considered a social problem in its own right or only if it results in poverty. Some argue that inequality itself is inherently problematic, as it undermines social cohesion, fosters resentment, and hampers economic mobility. Others contend that the primary concern should be poverty and the absolute deprivation of basic needs.

**Wilkinson and Pickett (2009)**, in their influential book "The Spirit Level," argue that high levels of inequality have detrimental effects on various social outcomes, including health, education, crime, and social mobility. They present evidence suggesting that more unequal societies tend to have worse health outcomes, higher rates of mental illness, and lower levels of social trust. According to Wilkinson and Pickett, reducing inequality is essential for improving overall societal well-being.

They argue that inequality affects everyone in society, not just the poorest. In more unequal societies, even the wealthier individuals experience worse health outcomes and lower levels of social trust compared to their counterparts in more equal societies. This perspective emphasizes the need to address inequality as a fundamental social problem that affects the overall quality of life for all members of society.

On the other hand, **Goldthorpe (2010)** provides a different perspective on inequality. He argues that the focus should be on poverty and social mobility rather than inequality per se. Goldthorpe suggests that the primary concern should be ensuring that individuals have the opportunity to move up the social ladder and improve their socio-economic status. From this viewpoint, inequality is not inherently problematic as long as there are sufficient opportunities for upward mobility and mechanisms to alleviate poverty.

Goldthorpe emphasizes the importance of education and employment opportunities in promoting social mobility. He argues that policies should focus on providing access to quality education, job training, and support for disadvantaged groups to enhance their chances of moving out of poverty and achieving economic success.

These differing perspectives highlight the complexity of the issue and the need for a nuanced understanding of inequality. While Wilkinson and Pickett emphasize the negative impacts of inequality on societal well-being, Goldthorpe focuses on the importance of addressing poverty and promoting social mobility as the primary goals.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, inequality is a significant social issue that affects various aspects of life and societal well-being. By measuring inequality using tools like the Gini coefficient and income quintile ratio, we can gain valuable insights into the distribution of resources within a society. The debate on whether inequality should be considered a social problem in its own right underscores the complexity of the issue.

Understanding the perspectives of scholars like Wilkinson and Pickett, who argue for the intrinsic harm of inequality, and Goldthorpe, who emphasizes the importance of poverty reduction and social mobility, allows us to develop a more comprehensive approach to addressing inequality. By integrating these viewpoints, we can create policies and interventions that promote both equity and opportunity, contributing to a more just and cohesive society.

### **Module 4: Key Concepts and Theoretical Perspectives**

#### **Narrative Lecture:**

**Social Stratification and Class**

Welcome to Module 4 of our lecture on social problems. Today, we will explore key concepts and theoretical perspectives that are essential for understanding social stratification, class, poverty, and inequality. These concepts provide the analytical tools necessary to examine how societies are structured and how individuals' positions within these structures affect their life chances and mobility.

Let's begin with **social stratification and class**. Social stratification refers to the hierarchical arrangement of individuals in a society based on various factors such as wealth, power, and prestige. This stratification creates layers or strata within a society, leading to unequal access to resources and opportunities.

There are three primary theoretical perspectives on social stratification and class: **Functionalist, Marxist, and Weberian perspectives**.

The **Functionalist perspective**, associated with sociologists like Émile Durkheim and Talcott Parsons, views stratification as a necessary and beneficial component of society. Functionalists argue that social stratification ensures that the most important positions are filled by the most qualified individuals. They believe that stratification incentivizes people to work harder and achieve more, thereby contributing to the overall stability and efficiency of society. According to this view, inequality is seen as a reflection of the differing levels of skill and effort among individuals.

In contrast, the **Marxist perspective**, rooted in the ideas of Karl Marx, sees social stratification as a source of conflict and exploitation. Marxists argue that stratification arises from the economic relationships in a capitalist society, where the bourgeoisie (owners of the means of production) exploit the proletariat (working class). This perspective highlights the inherent power imbalances and the resulting social inequality. Marxists believe that the capitalist system perpetuates class divisions and that the elimination of class structures is necessary to achieve a just and equitable society.

The **Weberian perspective**, developed by Max Weber, offers a more complex view of stratification. Weber acknowledges the importance of economic factors but also emphasizes the role of status and power in determining social stratification. According to Weber, social class is not only about economic resources but also about social prestige and political influence. Weber's approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how different forms of inequality intersect and influence individuals' positions within the social hierarchy.

**Social class** plays a crucial role in determining life chances and social mobility. Life chances refer to the opportunities individuals have to improve their quality of life and achieve their goals. Social class affects access to education, healthcare, employment, and other resources that are essential for upward mobility. Those in higher social classes typically have better life chances, while those in lower classes face more significant barriers to advancement.

**Social mobility** is the ability of individuals or groups to move up or down the social hierarchy. In societies with high social mobility, individuals have greater opportunities to improve their socio-economic status based on their efforts and achievements. Conversely, in societies with low social mobility, individuals' social positions are more rigid and determined by their birth and background. Understanding the factors that influence social mobility, such as education and economic policies, is crucial for addressing social inequality.

**Poverty and Inequality**

Next, let's explore the sociological perspectives on **poverty and inequality**. These perspectives help us understand the complex and interrelated nature of these social problems.

The **Structural-Functionalist perspective** views poverty and inequality as inevitable and necessary for the functioning of society. Functionalists argue that these conditions motivate individuals to work harder and fill various roles within the economy. They believe that inequality serves as an incentive for innovation and productivity, ultimately benefiting society as a whole.

The **Conflict perspective**, on the other hand, sees poverty and inequality as the result of power imbalances and exploitation. Conflict theorists argue that these conditions arise from the unequal distribution of resources and opportunities, perpetuated by those in power to maintain their dominance. This perspective highlights the systemic nature of poverty and inequality and calls for structural changes to address these issues.

The **Symbolic Interactionist perspective** focuses on the micro-level interactions and the meanings individuals attach to poverty and inequality. This approach examines how societal attitudes and stigmas affect individuals' experiences of poverty and inequality. Symbolic interactionists study how these social problems are constructed and perceived in everyday interactions and how these perceptions influence individuals' identities and behaviors.

**Globalization** has a significant impact on poverty and inequality. While globalization has led to economic growth and development in many parts of the world, it has also exacerbated inequalities both within and between countries. The global economy has created new opportunities for some but left others behind, leading to widening income gaps and social disparities.

Globalization has facilitated the flow of goods, services, capital, and labor across borders, resulting in economic integration and interdependence. However, the benefits of globalization have not been evenly distributed. Wealthier nations and individuals have often gained more, while poorer countries and marginalized communities have faced increased vulnerability and exploitation.

For example, multinational corporations can relocate production to countries with lower labor costs, maximizing profits but often at the expense of workers' rights and living conditions. This dynamic can lead to job losses and wage stagnation in higher-income countries while creating low-paying, precarious jobs in developing nations.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, understanding the key concepts and theoretical perspectives on social stratification, class, poverty, and inequality is essential for analyzing and addressing these social problems. By examining the Functionalist, Marxist, and Weberian perspectives on stratification and class, we gain insights into the underlying causes and consequences of social hierarchy. Sociological perspectives on poverty and inequality, along with the impact of globalization, provide a comprehensive framework for understanding these complex issues.

Lenore Walker developed the _cycle of abuse_ to explain why victims often remain in abusive relationships. The cycle has four stages:

- **Tension-building phase**
